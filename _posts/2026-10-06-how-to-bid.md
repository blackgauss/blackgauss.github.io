# How to Bid
*October 6, 2026*

Every production bidding system in the research literature factorizes the bid
the same way, and it's worth writing down why before any math:

```
bid(x) = multiplier · value(x),      value(x) = pCTR(x) · pCVR(x) · V
```

The `value` half is machine learning — calibrated probabilities learned from
your own logs, covered in a companion entry. This entry is about the
*multiplier* half: given a budget, a CPA target, an auction format, and a
competitive landscape, *what is the optimal function that turns value into a
bid?* The remarkable thing, after a decade of papers, is how settled the
answer is. The optimal shape is derivable; the constrained variants all
reduce to one structural theorem with more dual variables; and every
production system is some approximation of an online solver for one number
(λ) per constrained resource. I'll build the result up, then show where it's
still an open problem.

## Linear bidding is only the unconstrained special case

The heuristic that ran production for years (Zhang et al. 2016, eq. 1):

```
b(t) = b₀ · θₜ/θ̄
```

— bid proportional to the instance pCTR over the segment average; linear in
value, one tuned constant. And the default theory says: bid your value
(truthful), and you cannot be exploited. The central result of the bidding
literature is that **both are optimal only when nothing binds.** With a
budget or a CPA constraint, the value→bid map becomes nonlinear and the
nonlinearity is computable; and in a repeated auction with budgets, bidding
your true value isn't even dominant — Balseiro, Besbes & Weintraub
(*Management Science*, 2015) show optimal play there looks like *uniform
multiplicative pacing*: bid a fixed fraction α ≤ 1 of value, forever, so long
as the budget binds and never binds slack. Every DSP's throttling heuristic
had been reconstructing that equilibrium empirically since 2006. Constraints
force nonlinearity; the job is computing it.

## ORB: the derivation that settled the shape

Zhang, Yuan & Wang, "Optimal Real-Time Bidding for Display Advertising"
(KDD 2014), full derivation (their eqs. 5–16):

**Primal.** Maximize expected value (clicks) over a bidding *function* `b(θ)`
— not a bid, a function from KPI estimate θ to bid — subject to expected
spend ≤ B/N per auction opportunity arriving with density pθ(θ), where the
win-rate `w(b)` is the bid-landscape CDF:

```
max_b   ∫ θ·w(b(θ))·pθ(θ) dθ     s.t.   ∫ b(θ)·w(b(θ))·pθ(θ) dθ ≤ B/N
```

**KKT.** Lagrangian with multiplier λ, Euler–Lagrange derivative w.r.t.
`b(θ)`, and the objective collapses to one identity:

```
λ·w(b) = (θ − λ·b)·w′(b)
```

Read it English-first: *the marginal value of the budget (λ) equals the
marginal win-rate per cent, net of what you'd overpay by.* And note the
silence: **`pθ(θ)` cancels out.** The optimal bidding *function* depends only
on the win-rate curve and λ; the value distribution enters only by moving λ.
This is why the landscape is the load-bearing object, and why λ is a pacing
quantity (its own entry). Two parametric win-rate curves, two closed forms:

- `w(b) = b/(c+b)` (concave — fits most markets) ⇒ `b*(θ) = √(cθ/λ + c²) − c`.
- `w(b) = b²/(c²+b²)` (S-shaped — high-reserve markets) ⇒ a cubic in disguise;
  Cardano's difference-of-cube-roots closed form, or numerics.

Both are **concave in θ**: optimally you bid *more than proportionally* on
cheap traffic and *less than proportionally* on expensive — "buy many cheap
impressions, not few expensive ones" — because win-rate rises steepest at low
bids, so the marginal auction per dollar is cheapest there. (General form
`w(b)=b^α/(c^α+b^α)`: no analytic bid for α > 2, and the shape intuition
holds.) Finally λ itself is fixed by the budget-feasibility condition —
expected spend must equal B/N — which has no closed form, no problem,
bisection on a monotone function. **Three subsystems in one equation: the
value distribution from ML sets λ's level, the landscape sets the shape, the
budget sets λ through the spend equation.** The CPA-constrained variant adds
a second multiplier and the bid becomes a ratio of linear-fractional terms —
the shape argument is identical.

## USCB: every constrained problem is one formula

He et al. (Alibaba), "A Unified Solution to Constrained Bidding" (KDD 2021),
takes the natural generalization — an LP with impression-selection variables,
M side constraints (budget; CPA/ROI/CPC targets; deep-conversion ratios),
relaxed and dualized — and its Theorem 2.1 gives the optimal bid as:

```
bᵢ* = w₀*·vᵢ − Σⱼ wⱼ*·( qᵢⱼ(1 − 1_CRⱼ) − kⱼ·pᵢⱼ )        w₀* = 1/(α* + Σⱼ βⱼ*·1_CRⱼ)
```

English: **linear value scaling, minus per-constraint correction terms.** The
lovely corollary is what a CPA (cost-related) constraint contributes: the
additive correction vanishes (its indicator zeroes the sum) and only the
denominator inflates — **a CPA constraint is a tax on the multiplier, not a
filter on impressions.** Non-cost constraints (say, a ratio of conversions to
clicks) subtract value-dependent terms per impression. All the bookkeeping
reduces to K+1 dual numbers — solved offline with a real LP solver on
forecast supply, or online by dual ascent. When the paper's own ablations
compare against per-constraint heuristics, the wins are exactly where
constraints interact: a budget-tight campaign near its CPA ceiling needs
different value→bid curvature than either loop alone prescribes, and no
heuristic-family spans that interaction.

## The online solvers: feedback control of λ

λ must be solved *while the auctions run* — supply arrives online and
competitors drift. The map λ→spend has unknown, drifting *gain* (competition
moves under you), *dead time* (conversion delay), and multiplicative
response (steepening win-rate curves make spend move roughly exponentially
w.r.t. log-bid — pooled price distributions look log-normal, per-segment ones
fail the test). Predicted supply makes it a root-find; no forecast makes it a
control problem. Two published production answers:

**Feedback loops.** Zhang et al. (WSDM 2016, deployed on the BigTree mobile
DSP, offline-studied on iPinYou logs) ship bid
adjustment `b'(t) = b(t)·exp(φ(t))` — an exponential actuator keeping bids
positive — with φ from PID or **waterlevel** (integrator-only; φ(t+1) =
φ(t) + γ·(r − x(t))) control on the eCPC error. Their elegant §3.4 result:
set each channel's controller *reference* from the equalization condition —
at the optimal dual α, marginal clicks per yuan must be equal across
exchanges/geos, `(1/α) = cᵢ/cᵢ′ + ξᵢ` for all channels i — and a bank of
ignorant PID loops *becomes* the LP solution with automatic cross-channel
budget reallocation. Model-free optimization by setting references, not
solving programs. LinkedIn's 2014 pacer is the same family from the
budget side: a period LP computes the pacing rate, online correction tracks
it against realized spend — and its published failure lessons (overrun at
day boundaries; throttling as the reliable emergency brake because bid
changes have unpredictable spend response) are the reason throttling never
died.

**Dual descent, the modern canonical form.** Balseiro, Lu & Mirrokni, "The
Best of Many Worlds" (*Oper. Res.* 2023): keep the dual, act greedily against
**reduced value** `ṽᵢ = vᵢ − λ·cᵢ`, and update by online mirror descent — the
multiplicative rule `λ ← λ·exp(η·(cᵢ − B/T))/Z` and the subgradient rule are
instances of *one scheme* with different reference functions. Guarantees:
`Õ(√T)` regret under stochastic arrivals (optimal order), constant
competitive ratio adversarially, and **provably no early depletion** — λ
rises before the budget exhausts, the property every pacing engineer
actually cares about (Prop. 2). The survey literature (Aggarwal et al. 2024,
which I've read as the map of this whole area) states the consensus:
Lagrangian dual methods are "the work-horse algorithms of budget pacing" and
they *subsume* PID: the integral law in log-λ space **is** mirror descent
with a linear reference. Per-request O(1) — fits the bidder hot path exactly.

**The equilibrium those loops converge to, jointly.** When every bidder
runs a pacing multiplier, there's a fixed point: **first-price pacing
equilibrium** (Conitzer, Kroer, Sodomka & Stier-Moses; EC 2019 → *Management
Science* 2022): bids `αᵢ·vᵢⱼ` with `αᵢ ∈ [0,1]`, and the FPPE is
characterized as the *componentwise-maximal budget-feasible multiplier
vector* — feasible vectors closed under max implies uniqueness and
Pareto-dominance, and it's seller-revenue-maximizing too. Convergence: the
waterlevel law `λ ← λ·e^{η·err}` provably finds the period-optimal bid in
sublinear campaign time even under hidden, drifting cost functions
(Hajiaghayi & Springer 2022), and dual-descent dynamics converge to it in
repeated models (Balseiro, Kim, Mahdian, Mirrokni, *OR* 2021). The cold bath
at the end: computing the pacing equilibrium in general is **PPAD-hard**
(Chen, Kroer & Kumar, *Math. OR* 2023) — which is precisely why nobody solves
the fixed point and everybody runs feedback, which is precisely why the
control literature exists. State of the art now studies regret and efficiency
*without* assuming convergence at all (Gaitonde et al., ITCS 2023): the loop
need not settle to be good.

## Where RL actually sits

Cai et al. (WSDM 2017) formulated the day as an MDP — state = (time,
remaining budget, request features), action = bid, reward = click/conversion —
and solved it with deep Q-learning over coarsely-discretized budget states,
reporting double-digit lifts over landscape-only baselines on iPinYou/YOYI;
multi-advertiser extensions followed (Jin et al. CIKM 2018). The useful
positioning comes from the comparison view: **ORB/USCB solve the open-loop
problem** (optimal function under an *estimated* landscape and known λ
mechanics), while **RL solves the closed-loop one** — the environment is
partly *you*, your own pacing perturbs the landscape, and offline logs are
selection-biased by your past policy (you observe only the auctions you
won, at the prices you paid). The bandit-theory framing closes the loop
formally: budgeted bidding is bandits-with-knapsacks, and Balseiro & Gur
(*Management Science* 2019) give regret-optimal learning procedures in
repeated budgeted auctions — the correspondence between *learning well* and
*equilibrium behavior* is now theorem-grade. The honest read of five or six
years of RL-bidding papers: where the state and reward are definable, RL
wins modestly and expensively; where they aren't (sparse conversions,
nonstationary markets, a policy that changes the data it trains on), the LP
dual decomposition + online dual updates is simpler, provable, and
production-hardened. The winning published production systems are
characterized by *simple policy classes with many dual weights and robust
online solvers* — the bid map should be derivable, not just learned.

## First-price markets: the multiplier survives, the shade goes inside

Under first price, payment = bid, and the whole machinery re-expresses:
per-auction, maximize `(v − b)·W(b)`, whose first-order condition,
`v − b* = W(b*)/w(b*)`, sets the shade as the inverse hazard rate of market
price (the shading entry takes this apart; the empirical WWW'24 studies show
market-wide play is *mostly uniform* shading, i.e., FPPE's prediction holds
approximately in production and deviations concentrate in thin and thick
auctions). And the structural news from USCB's generalization: the
bid = `min(λ_budget, λ_ROS) · shade(x) · v` composition shows **the dual
structure survives the format change** — pacing and shading are two multipliers
on the same channel, which is the trap its entry describes.

## The bidder's design checklist, as this thread composes into a system

1. One bid function: `bid = f(v̂(x), λ, duals, shade(x))`; derive it, don't
   learn it, then tune it.
2. Constrained resources each get one dual: budget, CPA, ROS, frequency
   caps, min-delivery floors. Parallel loops, **min-compose the multipliers**
   — serial cascading double-penalizes and violates constraints by Ω(T)
   (Balseiro et al., KDD 2023's field guide proves the separation; the paper
   calls the architecture the whole difference between guarantee and
   disaster).
3. Landscape models (`w(b|x)`, `p(z|x)`) are offline artifacts feeding both
   the bid shape and the shade; calibration is a first-class output, not a
   final-stage polish.
4. λ updated by multiplicative dual descent with anti-windup, gain
   scheduling from `dS/dφ` estimates (the landscape gives them for free), and
   never zeroed on staleness — last-known-good with conservative bias, or you
   turn off a line-item by an outage of a cache miss.
5. Explore deliberately (budgeted bandits), because your censoring means
   unexplored segments are un-estimable, and the map is driftier than your
   priors.
6. Instrument the *derived* quantities: realized CPA vs target per channel,
   marginal clicks/yuan equalization residuals, λ trajectories per campaign.
   Revenue tells you last week; these tell you today.

The satisfaction of this thread, for me, is architectural: what began as
"guess a bid" is one convex story — a value model learned from logs, a
landscape learned from censoring, a dual price solved by feedback — with
proofs attached at the interesting corners. It is rare for a market to be
this legible from the buyer's seat, and that legibility is itself the reason
the moat (from the economy entry) moved from algorithms to data.
